# A3 — The allocation LP

Generated by `python -m analysis.allocate`. Maximises season revenue over tiles-per-crop and
animal counts, subject to land, labour, per-product market depth, the shed cap, and the wheat
balance that feeds the animals. Concave revenue and convex purchase cost are represented as
piecewise-linear segments, so the LP fills high-price segments first without integer variables.

## The optimum

100 tiles available, sweeping hired hands and travel overhead:

| travel | hands | revenue | land used | labour used | allocation |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1.00 | 6 | $97,225 | 94/100 | 157/168 | wheat 37, tomato 16, **goose 16**, melon 11, strawberry 7, carrot 4, sheep 2, cow 2 |
| 1.25 | 7 | $96,672 | 89/100 | **192/192** | wheat 40, **goose 16**, melon 11, tomato 9, strawberry 6, carrot 4, sheep 2, cow 2 |
| 1.50 | 9 | $95,593 | 94/100 | 235/240 | wheat 37, tomato 16, **goose 16**, melon 11, strawberry 7, carrot 4, sheep 2, cow 2 |

**The result is robust to travel overhead.** Between a 0% and a 50% walking tax the optimum
moves by 1.7% of revenue and three hires; the allocation barely changes. Movement was the
biggest unmodelled gap after A2 and it turns out not to matter much — worth knowing before
spending effort on pathfinding in Phase B.

## This corrects A2

A2 concluded *land* is the binding constraint. **At the optimum it is labour.** Land runs
89–94% utilised while worker-turns saturate.

A2's capacity arithmetic was right — labour binds below ~10 hands, land above — but its
implication was wrong. It reasoned "hiring is cheap, so hire past the crossover and land
becomes the constraint." That ignored two things the LP prices properly:

1. Hire cost is Fibonacci **and paid every day**. The 12th hand costs $376/day, $9,024 across
   a season.
2. Marginal revenue per worker collapses once the shallow pools are drained. There is nothing
   profitable left for the 12th worker to do.

So the optimum stops at 6–9 hands, where labour is saturated and land is not.

## Buy two quadrants, not three

| land | revenue | unlock cost | net |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 | $64,589 | $0 | $64,589 |
| 50 | $83,203 | $1,000 | $82,203 |
| 75 | $95,294 | $3,000 | **$92,294** |
| 100 | $96,672 | $7,000 | $89,672 |

The third expansion costs $4,000 and returns $1,378. **Skip it.** Once labour binds, extra land
is nearly worthless — the same reason the optimum leaves 10% of the board idle at 75 tiles.

This also corrects A2's "buy land aggressively, $7,000 for 4× the ceiling."

## Grow feed, don't buy it

The LP buys **zero** wheat. This was A3's opening question and the answer is unambiguous: with
wheat's below-`I0` curve being `sqrt` at target 0.80, purchased feed escalates faster than the
land it frees is worth. Grow it.

Every shallow pool is drained to near its cap (melon 143/158, fertilizer 451/493, wool 50/59),
with eggs carrying the unbounded remainder. **The optimum is a diversified raid, not a
monoculture** — it takes almost all of every finite pool and then runs geese for the rest of
the season. That is a direct consequence of A1's finding that pool depth, not base price, sets
a product's value.

Note the 16-goose figure is not a coincidence: 20 animals × 24 days ≈ 480 fertilizer, which is
the 493-unit pool. The animal count is set by the *fertilizer* pool, not by egg economics.

## Caveats

1. **Upper bound, not a plan.** The LP assumes capital is available when needed. You start with
   $3,000; the allocation above needs ~$7,000 of livestock. Bootstrapping is unmodelled, so
   real revenue will be lower and the early game is a sequencing problem the LP cannot answer.
2. **Continuous relaxation.** Tiles and animals are fractional; melon's 143 units assumes
   cycles pack perfectly into 24 days, where integer cycles give ~132.
3. **Single-agent.** No opponent competes for the shallow pools. Since melon, wool and
   fertilizer are drained to near their caps here, a contesting opponent roughly halves those
   lines. That is A4's question.
4. **Travel is a flat multiplier**, not real pathfinding — justified above by the result's
   insensitivity to it.
